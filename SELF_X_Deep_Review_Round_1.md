# HIGH LEVEL SUMMARY
---
## Summary
This paper introduces SELF-X, a three-timescale control-plane decomposition for software-defined networks (SDNs) designed to reconcile the multi-second inference latency of large language model (LLM) agents with sub-second switch buffer saturation dynamics. The architecture separates control into a 100 ms fast analytic Lyapunov drift-plus-penalty (DPP) optimizer, a 5 s mid-timescale local LoRA-adapted small language model (SmolLM2-135M) policy head, and a 30 s slow-timescale multi-agent remote LLM governor that adjusts objective parameters and action masks $\Delta\pi = (V, \mathbf{w}, \mathcal{M})$ rather than emitting individual flow rules. Evaluated across a six-stage incremental ablation on a bit-conserving two-path fluid simulator with live free-tier API latency and quota-fallback charging, the full pipeline reduces mean delay by 44.4% and packet loss by 36.0% compared to static OpenFlow forwarding.

## Strengths
* **Rigorous Control-Plane Latency Charging and Loss-Floor Accounting**: The paper introduces an exceptionally honest evaluation methodology by charging measured remote LLM API latency, rate-limit pacing, and multi-provider quota fallback walks directly as datapath actuation lag ($\lceil L/\tau \rceil$ slots), while explicitly separating structurally unavoidable capacity-overload drops ($\ell^\star = 7.97\%$) from controller-addressable loss.
* **Latency-Grounded Timescale Decomposition with Analytic Stability**: Replacing cold-start reinforcement learning in the fast loop with a 33.1 $\mu\text{s}$ analytic Lyapunov DPP optimizer avoids unsafe online exploration and confines the slow language-model layers to adjusting objective weights and action masks rather than installing raw datapath rules.
* **Exemplary Transparency and Reproducibility Contract**: The authors provide a deterministic record-and-replay JSONL trace of all live LLM queries alongside exact bit-conservation assertions, and they candidly report negative and partial results—including that the multi-agent governance stage does not uniformly outperform the LoRA stage and that zero-shot workload transfer retains only 73.0% of the training reward gain.

## Weaknesses
* **Missing Standalone Analytic Lyapunov DPP Baseline**: Because the six-stage ablation introduces the 100 ms Lyapunov DPP loop last (at Stage S5, on top of the LoRA and LLM governance layers) and accounts for nearly the entire performance jump, please consider evaluating a standalone fixed-parameter Lyapunov DPP baseline ($V=8.0$, static weights $\mathbf{w}$, unmasked action set $\mathcal{A}$) to verify whether the 5 s LoRA and 30 s LLM governance layers provide any net improvement over the classical 100 ms analytic controller alone.
* **Unspecified Algorithmic Coupling Between the Three Loops Across Stages S3–S5**: Neither Algorithm 1, Algorithm 2, nor Figure 1 specifies how the 5 s mid-loop LoRA policy head's window-level action token interacts with the 100 ms fast-loop DPP optimizer in Stage S5 (which appears to independently select slot-level actions from the 30 s governor's mask $\mathcal{M}$), nor how the governor's $(V, \mathbf{w})$ updates affect Stage S4 before the fast loop is active; the authors might consider formalizing these inter-loop interfaces.
* **Theoretical Qualifications on the Theorem 1 Backlog Bound**: The proof sketch of Theorem 1 assumes the $\varepsilon$-slack stationary randomized policy remains admissible at every slot, which can be invalidated when the slow loop masks out `reroute` or when the downstream deterministic safety arbiter vetoes actions into `no_op`, and also exhibits minor dimensional inconsistencies regarding the slot duration factor $\tau$ and finite-buffer clipping.
* **Incremental Novelty of Individual Architectural Mechanisms and Modest Empirical Gains from Weight Distillation**: The slow $\Delta\pi$ multi-agent governance channel and offline reflection-to-SLM weight distillation closely adapt recent paradigms (Reference Paper: Multi-Agent LLM Governance for Safe Two-Timescale Reinforcement Learning in SDN-IoT Defense, Saeid Jamshidi et al., 2026; Reference Paper: Reflection-Driven Self-Improving Language Model Agents for Adaptive RAN Slicing, Yuanhao Li et al., 2026; Reference Paper: NetLLM: Adapting Large Language Models for Networking, Duo Wu et al., 2024; Reference Paper: An Agentic Approach for Dynamic Software-Defined Network Management Using Large Language Models, Aramis Sales Araujo et al., 2024), and distilling over a small 24-state replay buffer slightly degrades delay and loss in Stage S3 compared to prompt-based self-critique in Stage S2.
* **Reporting Discrepancies and Simplified Fluid Simulation Scope**: The empirical evaluation relies on a simplified two-path fluid simulator rather than a packet-level testbed with TCP feedback, and exhibits several reporting inconsistencies—such as listing `331.0 ms` instead of `0.033 ms` (`33.1 µs`) for SELF-X control latency in Table IV, omitting the promised seed-level cluster bootstrap confidence intervals in Table V, and conflicting statements between Sections V-E and VI-C on whether rate-limit pacing is charged in $L$.

## Potential Issues And Suggestions
### [Framing, Motivation, and Literature Positioning] (Pages: 1-2, 9)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Unstated Excess-Arrival Rate Assumption in the Motivating Buffer-Saturation Calculation (Section I, Page 1)**
   - **Potential Concern:** To motivate the latency gap in Section I (Page 1, Column 2), the paper states: "a congested 10 Mbps link with a 2 Mbit buffer saturates in about 200 ms."
   - **Substantiation:** By the fluid queue evolution equation in Equation (1) ($\tilde{Q}_i(t) = Q_i(t) + (\lambda_i(t) - C_i)\tau$), the time required for an initially empty buffer of size $B_{\text{buf}} = 2.0\text{ Mbit}$ to saturate is $B_{\text{buf}} / (\lambda_i(t) - C_i)$, which depends on the *net excess arrival rate* $\lambda_i(t) - C_i$ above the link capacity $C_i = 10\text{ Mbps}$, rather than on the link capacity $C_i$ itself. A saturation time of $200\text{ ms}$ ($2.0\text{ Mbit} / 0.2\text{ s} = 10\text{ Mbps}$) holds specifically when the instantaneous arrival rate $\lambda_i(t)$ is $20\text{ Mbps}$ (a $2\times$ link overload creating a $10\text{ Mbps}$ net queue accumulation rate), whereas dividing $B_{\text{buf}} = 2\text{ Mbit}$ directly by $C_i = 10\text{ Mbps}$ computes the buffer *drain* time (maximum queueing delay of a full buffer), not the buffer *fill* time unless the excess arrival rate equals $C_i$.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** State the assumed overload rate explicitly in Section I (e.g., "...under a 20 Mbps burst (a 10 Mbps excess arrival rate), a 10 Mbps link with a 2 Mbit buffer saturates in 200 ms") so the motivating queueing arithmetic is self-contained and does not appear to conflate buffer drain time with buffer fill time.

2. **Unexplained Composition of the 135,000 Control Slots Claimed in the Abstract and Section VI-B (Abstract, Page 1; Section VI-B, Page 5)**
   - **Potential Concern:** The Abstract states that the six-stage incremental pipeline is evaluated "across 5 paired seeds and 135,000 control slots," and Section VI-B (Page 5) similarly states "Every configuration is run on 5 seeds (1900, 1901, 1902, 1903, 1904), 60 windows of 5 s each, i.e. 300 s and 3000 slots per run, for 135,000 control slots in total." Because each run consists of $60\text{ windows} \times 5\text{ s} / 0.1\text{ s} = 3,000\text{ control slots}$ ($5 \times 3,000 = 15,000\text{ slots}$ per configuration), evaluating 5 seeds across the 6 pipeline stages (`S0`–`S5`) accounts for $6 \times 5 \times 3,000 = 90,000\text{ control slots}$. The 135,000 figure requires 9 configurations ($9 \times 5 \times 3,000 = 135,000$), which implicitly sums the 6 pipeline stages (`S0`–`S5`) and the 3 external non-LLM reference baselines introduced in Section VI-A.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Specify in the Abstract and Section VI-B that the 135,000 control slots span the six pipeline stages plus three external non-LLM reference baselines (9 configurations in total) so the slot accounting is immediately transparent to a reader checking the arithmetic.

### [System Model, Fluid Datapath Dynamics, and Lyapunov Stability Formulation] (Pages: 2-4)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Bufferless Formulation of the Claimed "Irreducible Loss Floor" $\ell^\star$ in Equation (7) vs. Buffered Queueing Dynamics (Section III-D)**
   - **Potential Concern:** In Section III-D (Page 3), the paper states that in slots where the superposed demand $m(t) + e(t)$ exceeds total capacity $C_0 + C_1$, *"no work-conserving controller, however clever, can avoid a drop,"* and defines the irreducible loss floor in Equation (7) as:
     $$\ell^\star = \frac{\sum_t \big[(m(t) + e(t)) - \min(m(t) + e(t), C_0 + C_1)\big] \tau}{\sum_t (m(t) + e(t)) \tau}.$$
     Equation (7) computes the excess arrival volume over the aggregate service rate $C_0 + C_1$ on an instantaneous, memoryless (bufferless, $B_{\text{buf}} = 0$) basis. However, in the buffered datapath model of Equations (1)–(3), each path has a finite buffer of capacity $B_{\text{buf}} = 2.0\text{ Mbit}$ ($2 B_{\text{buf}} = 4.0\text{ Mbit}$ total across both paths). When an arrival burst satisfies $m(t) + e(t) > C_0 + C_1 = 20\text{ Mbps}$ during a slot of duration $\tau = 100\text{ ms}$, the excess bits $\big(m(t) + e(t) - (C_0 + C_1)\big)\tau$ are first absorbed into the available queue buffer headroom $2 B_{\text{buf}} - (Q_0(t) + Q_1(t))$ rather than dropped immediately, and can subsequently be drained in quieter slots whenever $m(t') + e(t') < C_0 + C_1$ with zero tail-drop overflow $o_i(t)$. A tail-drop overflow is unavoidable only after cumulative burst volume exceeds the remaining buffer headroom.
   - **Discrete Routing Granularity vs. Pooled Capacity:** Conversely, because the discrete action set $\mathcal{A} = \{\text{no\_op}, \text{reroute}, \text{rate\_limit}\}$ moves the entire elephant aggregate $e(t)$ as an indivisible unit rather than splitting it fractionally across both paths within a single slot, no single action in $\mathcal{A}$ can simultaneously utilize the full $C_0 + C_1$ capacity if $m(t) < C_0$ while $e(t)$ overflows the single path onto which it is steered.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** To make the loss accounting mathematically exact, the authors should either: (a) compute the stateful buffered minimum-loss floor via a pooled work-conserving queue of service rate $C_0 + C_1 = 20\text{ Mbps}$ and aggregate buffer capacity $2 B_{\text{buf}} = 4.0\text{ Mbit}$ evolving over the trace:
     $$\tilde{Q}_{\text{tot}}(t) = Q_{\text{tot}}(t) + \big(m(t) + e(t) - (C_0 + C_1)\big)\tau, \quad Q_{\text{tot}}(t+1) = \min\!\big(2 B_{\text{buf}}, \max(0, \tilde{Q}_{\text{tot}}(t))\big),$$
     summing the resulting overflow $\max(0, \tilde{Q}_{\text{tot}}(t) - 2 B_{\text{buf}})$ over $t$, or (b) explicitly qualify $\ell^\star$ in Section III-D as a *bufferless instantaneous capacity-excess benchmark* rather than a strict trajectory-level lower bound for buffered controllers.

2. **Missing Explicit Mathematical Definitions for Action-to-Rate Mapping $\lambda_i(a)$, Pre-Queue Metered Loss Accounting, and Operating Cost $c(a)$ (Sections III-B, III-C, and IV, Equation 10)**
   - **Potential Concern:** In Section IV (Page 3), the per-slot fast-loop decision rule in Equation (10) (and Line 1 of Algorithm 1 on Page 4) selects:
     $$a^\star(t) = \arg\min_{a \in \mathcal{M}} \left[ \sum_i Q_i(t)\big(\lambda_i(a) - C_i\big) + V c(a) \right]$$
     over $\mathcal{M} \subseteq \mathcal{A} = \{\text{no\_op}, \text{reroute}, \text{rate\_limit}\}$. While the conceptual roles of the actions and costs are described in prose, three functional specifications required to evaluate Equation (10) and reproduce the fast-loop optimizer from the manuscript text are omitted:
     1. *Piecewise action-to-arrival-rate mapping $\lambda_i(a)$ and synthesis order of $r$:* Section III-A specifies that the mice aggregate $m(t)$ stays on the primary path ($i=0$) while the elephant aggregate $e(t)$ is the only traffic the controller may move or meter, and Section III-B notes that a `rate_limit` action admits $\min(e(t), r/1000)\text{ Mbps}$. However, the explicit piecewise formulas mapping each $a \in \mathcal{A}$ to $(\lambda_0(a), \lambda_1(a))$ are not written out—specifically whether `no_op` sets $(\lambda_0, \lambda_1) = (m(t) + e(t), 0)$, `reroute` sets $(\lambda_0, \lambda_1) = (m(t), e(t))$, and `rate_limit` keeps the metered elephant flow on path $0$ as $(\lambda_0, \lambda_1) = (m(t) + \min(e(t), r/1000), 0)$ (as well as how $r = \min(0.9 C_0 - m(t), 0.85 e(t)) \cdot 1000$ handles the case $m(t) > 0.9 C_0$). Furthermore, in Algorithm 1 (Page 4, Lines 1–4), $a^\star$ is selected via Equation (10) on Line 1 *before* the meter rate $r$ is synthesized on Line 3 inside `if a* = rate_limit then`, even though evaluating $\lambda_0(\text{rate\_limit})$ and $c(\text{rate\_limit})$ inside the $\arg\min$ on Line 1 requires knowing $r$ beforehand.
     2. *Pre-queue metered loss in bit conservation and drift:* Equations (1)–(4) define queue evolution and bit conservation $Q_i(t) + \lambda_i(t)\tau = s_i(t) + o_i(t) + Q_i(t+1)$ solely in terms of the post-meter steered arrival rate $\lambda_i(t)$ and tail-drop buffer overflow $o_i(t) = \max(0, \tilde{Q}_i(t) - B_{\text{buf}})$. While Section III-B states that *"metered bits are counted as loss, not hidden,"* the pre-queue metered discard $m_{\text{drop}}(t) = \max(0, e(t) - r/1000)\tau \cdot \mathbb{I}(a(t) = \text{rate\_limit})$ does not appear in Equations (1)–(4) or in an explicit formula for window loss $\ell_w$ in Equation (6). Moreover, because `rate_limit` discards traffic prior to the queue, the drift term $\sum_i Q_i(t)(\lambda_i(a) - C_i)$ is strictly smaller under `rate_limit` than under `no_op` whenever $Q_0(t) > 0$; thus, the exact formulation of the loss penalty inside $c(\text{rate\_limit})$ is critical to understanding how the optimizer avoids trivially favoring `rate_limit` whenever queues build up.
     3. *Algebraic specification of the operating cost $c(a)$:* Section IV states verbally that *"The cost $c(a)$ is the weighted sum of normalised delay, loss, energy and resource terms minus normalised throughput, with weights $\mathbf{w}$ supplied by the slow loop,"* bounded in $[0, C_{\max}]$. Unlike the window reward $R_w$ in Equation (6)—which is given explicitly with numerical normalization constants—none of the five prospective per-slot components of $c(a)$ (especially the "energy" and "resource" proxy terms, which do not appear anywhere else in Section III or Table II), their normalization constants, the initial value of $\mathbf{w}$ and $C_{\max}$, or how subtracting normalized throughput is shifted or clipped to preserve $c(a) \ge 0$ are mathematically defined.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Provide the explicit piecewise equations for $(\lambda_0(a), \lambda_1(a))$, the formula for total slot/window loss combining tail-drop $o_i(t)$ and pre-queue metered bits, and the algebraic expression for the five components of $c(a)$.

3. **Theoretical Qualifications on Theorem 1 Under Restricted Action Masks $\mathcal{M}$, Safety Arbiter Vetoes, Finite-Buffer Clipping, Overload Bursts, and Time-Varying Governance Parameters (Section IV, Theorem 1)**
   - **Potential Concern:** Theorem 1 (Page 3, Equation 11) and its concluding discussion at the top of Page 4 state that moving $V$, $\mathbf{w}$, and the action mask $\mathcal{M}$ in the slow loop *"moves the trade-off along the curve the theorem describes; it cannot invalidate the bound."* There are four structural subtleties between the hypotheses/proof sketch of Theorem 1 and the system model in Sections III–V that warrant explicit qualification:
     1. *Restriction to the masked action set $\mathcal{M}$, pre-queue metering, and safety arbiter vetoes:* The proof sketch argues that because Equation (10) minimizes the right-hand side over $\mathcal{M}$ slot by slot, *"it is no worse than the $\varepsilon$-slack stationary randomised policy, whose drift term is at most $-\varepsilon \sum_i Q_i(t)$."* This inequality holds only if the stationary randomized policy achieving capacity-region slack $\varepsilon > 0$ is supported entirely within the restricted admissible subset $\mathcal{M} \subseteq \mathcal{A}$ permitted by the slow loop and is not subsequently overridden by the deterministic safety arbiter (Algorithm 1, Line 5). Under the slow-loop mask gate, any subset of size $|\mathcal{M}| \ge 2$ containing `no_op` is admissible—including $\mathcal{M} = \{\text{no\_op}, \text{rate\_limit}\}$, which excludes `reroute` and leaves the alternate path capacity $C_1 = 10\text{ Mbps}$ unutilized. Similarly, whenever the safety arbiter vetoes `reroute` or `rate_limit` into `no_op`, the executed action no longer minimizes Equation (10). If arrival rates exceed $C_0 = 10\text{ Mbps}$, no work-conserving routing policy within $\mathcal{M} = \{\text{no\_op}, \text{rate\_limit}\}$ (or under an arbiter veto to `no_op`) can stabilize path $0$, and queue stability can only be achieved through pre-queue packet drops (`rate_limit` admission control) or buffer tail-drop clipping.
     2. *Consistency of the $\varepsilon$-slack capacity premise with periodic overload bursts:* Theorem 1 assumes $\mathbb{E}[\mu_i(t) - \lambda_i(t)] \ge \varepsilon > 0$ under a stationary randomized policy. However, Section III-D notes that the offered workload contains superposed elephant bursts whose instantaneous demand exceeds total capacity $C_0 + C_1$ ($\ell^\star = 7.97\%$). Section IV should clarify whether the $\varepsilon$-slack condition is assumed to hold for the long-run stationary mean arrival rate under work-conserving routing ($\{\text{no\_op}, \text{reroute}\}$) or whether it relies on pre-queue metering (`rate_limit`) during bursts.
     3. *Finite-buffer clipping vs. the infinite-buffer analytic ceiling:* Because Equation (2) hard-clips each backlog at $B_{\text{buf}} = 2.0\text{ Mbit}$ on every slot, the total backlog is deterministically bounded by $\sum_{i \in \{0,1\}} Q_i(t) \le 2 B_{\text{buf}} = 4.0\text{ Mbit}$ for *all* $t$ under any control policy (including `no_op`), by exchanging queue growth for tail-drop overflow $o_i(t)$. While Section VII-E candidly notes that the physical buffer binds before the analytic ceiling $(B + V C_{\max})/\varepsilon$, Section IV should state directly after Theorem 1 that Equation (11) characterizes the unclipped queue process (or the regime where $(B + V C_{\max})/\varepsilon < 2 B_{\text{buf}}$).
     4. *Telescoping across governance epochs with time-varying $(V, \mathbf{w}, \mathcal{M})$:* The telescoping sum over $T \to \infty$ slots in the proof sketch assumes a fixed trade-off weight $V$, a stationary cost function $c(t) \in [0, C_{\max}]$, and a fixed mask $\mathcal{M}$. Because the slow loop updates $(V, \mathbf{w}, \mathcal{M})$ every $T_g = 30\text{ s}$ (with $V \in [0.5, 32]$), telescoping across epochs requires either stating that Theorem 1 holds piecewise for a fixed governance tuple $(V, \mathbf{w}, \mathcal{M})$ or bounding the telescoped penalty term using $V_{\max} = 32$ across stabilizing masks.

4. **Inconsistent Service Rate Notation ($\mu_i(t)$ vs. $C_i$), Missing Slot Duration Factor $\tau$ in the Drift Inequality, and Telemetry Quantization (Section III-C and Section IV, Equations 9–11)**
   - **Service Rate Notation and Dimensional Consistency of $\tau$:** In Table II and Equation (1), queue backlog $Q_i(t)$ is in $\text{Mbit}$, arrival and service rates $\lambda_i(t), C_i$ are in $\text{Mbps}$ ($\text{Mbit/s}$), and slot duration is $\tau = 100\text{ ms} = 0.1\text{ s}$, so the per-slot change $(\lambda_i(t) - C_i)\tau$ in Equation (1) has units of $\text{Mbit}$. Equation (10) likewise uses the deterministic capacity $C_i$. However, Equation (9), the definition of $B \triangleq \frac{1}{2}\sum_i [(\lambda_i^{\max}\tau)^2 + (\mu_i^{\max}\tau)^2]$, and Theorem 1 introduce an undefined stochastic process $\mu_i(t)$ (and $\mu_i^{\max}$) in place of $C_i$. Furthermore, squaring Equation (1), $\tilde{Q}_i(t) = Q_i(t) + (\lambda_i(t) - C_i)\tau$, yields the exact one-slot quadratic expansion:
     $$\frac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}_i(t)^2 - \frac{1}{2}Q_i(t)^2 = \frac{1}{2}\big(\lambda_i(t) - C_i\big)^2 \tau^2 - Q_i(t)\big(C_i - \lambda_i(t)\big)\tau.$$
     Equations (9) and (10) omit the multiplying factor $\tau$ from the linear drift cross-term $\sum_i Q_i(t)\mathbb{E}[\mu_i(t) - \lambda_i(t) \mid \mathbf{Q}(t)]$ while retaining $\tau^2$ inside $B$ (which has units of $\text{Mbit}^2$). If $\varepsilon = 0.6$ in Table II (listed without units) is defined via $\mathbb{E}[\mu_i(t) - \lambda_i(t)] \ge \varepsilon$ in $\text{Mbps}$, then $B/\varepsilon$ in Equation (11) has mismatched units and is off by a factor of $\tau = 0.1\text{ s}$ unless $\varepsilon$ and $V$ are explicitly scaled by $\tau$ (or $\varepsilon$ is expressed in $\text{Mbit/slot}$). Unifying $\mu_i(t) \equiv C_i$, specifying the units of $\varepsilon$ in Table II, and tracking $\tau$ consistently across Equations (9)–(11) would resolve this inconsistency.
   - **Telemetry Poller Quantization in Equation (10):** Section III-C states that telemetry is reported to the control plane at a declared poller resolution ($1.0\text{ Mbps}$ on mice, $2.5\text{ Mbps}$ on the elephant aggregate, and $1.0\text{ Mbit}$ on backlogs) and that *"the controller never sees more precision than a real statistics poller would give it."* However, Equation (10), Theorem 1, and Algorithm 1 are written in terms of the exact continuous state variables $Q_i(t), m(t), e(t)$. Clarifying whether the $100\text{ ms}$ fast loop evaluates Equation (10) using quantized poller observations $(\hat{Q}_i(t), \hat{m}(t), \hat{e}(t))$ (which introduces a bounded quantization error term into the drift constant $B$ in Equation 9 and leaves the queue-drift weight zero whenever $Q_i(t) < 0.5\text{ Mbit}$ under nearest-integer rounding) or whether the declared poller resolution applies only to the windowed telemetry $z$ passed to the slower loops would remove an ambiguity in the system model.

### [Three-Timescale SELF-X Architecture, Algorithms, and Experimental Protocol] (Pages: 4-6)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Undefined Inter-Loop Control Flow Between the Mid-Loop LoRA Policy Head ($T_w = 5\text{ s}$) and the Slow/Fast Loops ($T_g = 30\text{ s}$, $\tau = 100\text{ ms}$) Across Stages S1–S5 (Abstract & Sections I–II, Pages 1–2; Section V, Figure 1, and Algorithms 1–2, Page 4; Section VI-A, Page 5; Sections VII-B and VIII, Pages 6–7):**
   A central claim of the Abstract, Sections I–II, Section V, and Section VI-A is that SELF-X operates as a three-timescale decomposition ($\tau = 100\text{ ms}$ fast loop, $T_w = 5\text{ s}$ mid-loop LoRA policy head, and $T_g = 30\text{ s}$ slow-loop multi-agent governor) evaluated via a strictly incremental six-stage ablation (S0 through S5), where each stage adds exactly one mechanism to the preceding stage. However, a potential concern regarding architectural clarity and reproducibility is that the manuscript framing, Section V, Figure 1, and Algorithms 1–2 do not specify how the three loops interact in Stages S4 and S5, nor do they fully specify the active decision paths across Stages S1–S4 prior to the introduction of the fast loop at Stage S5:
   - **Disconnect in Figure 1 and Algorithms 1–2 (Role of the Mid Loop in Stage S5):** Figure 1 (p. 4) depicts only two timescales ("Slow timescale — governance epoch 30 s" and "Fast timescale — control slot $\tau = 100\text{ ms}$") connected by "the single channel between them," $\Delta\pi = (V, \mathbf{w}, \mathcal{M})$. While the "LoRA adapter $r=8, \alpha=16$" appears as a block inside the $30\text{ s}$ slow-timescale container in Figure 1, Section V-B defines the LoRA policy head as the mid loop running every $T_w = 5\text{ s}$ and decoding one of the three discrete action tokens (`no_op`, `reroute`, `rate_limit`) for the coming $5\text{ s}$ window. Conversely, in Stage S5, the fast loop (Algorithm 1, Line 1) independently computes $a^\star \leftarrow \arg\min_{a \in \mathcal{M}} [\sum_i Q_i(t)(\lambda_i(a) - C_i) + V c(a)]$ at every $100\text{ ms}$ slot and installs $a$ via the safety arbiter (Lines 5–6). Algorithm 2 (Line 16) returns $(V, \mathbf{w}, \mathcal{M})$ directly to the fast loop, and neither Algorithm 1 nor Algorithm 2 takes an input from or passes an output to the $5\text{ s}$ LoRA policy head. It is therefore unclear what functional role the $5\text{ s}$ LoRA head plays in Stage S5 once the $100\text{ ms}$ analytic optimizer takes over slot-level action selection—specifically, whether the $100\text{ ms}$ fast loop completely overrides/bypasses the $5\text{ s}$ LoRA head, uses the LoRA action to restrict $\mathcal{M}$ or modify $c(a)$, or how S5 constitutes a strictly additive increment over S3/S4 if slot-level analytic optimization replaces window-level LoRA decoding.
   - **Role of $\Delta\pi = (V, \mathbf{w}, \mathcal{M})$ in Stage S4 (Without the Fast Loop):** Section VI-A (p. 5) defines Stage S4 as adding "six-agent governance of $(V, \mathbf{w}, \mathcal{M})$" on top of Stage S3 (the LoRA policy head), whereas the fast Lyapunov loop that consumes $V$ and $\mathbf{w}$ in Eq. 3. **Edge Cases in Local Rate-Limit Synthesis (Algorithm 1, Lines 1–5) and Weight Vector Normalization (Algorithm 2, Lines 6–8):**
   Two algorithmic specification details on Page 4 warrant clarification:
   - **Local Rate-Limit Synthesis and Safety Arbiter Interaction (Algorithm 1, Lines 1–5):** When $a^\star = \texttt{rate\_limit}$, Algorithm 1 (Line 3) and the mid loop (Section V-B) synthesize the meter rate locally as $r \leftarrow \min(0.9 C_0 - m(t),\; 0.85 e(t)) \cdot 1000\text{ [kbps]}$. If mice traffic $m(t)$ ever exceeds $8.488\text{ Mbps}$ (so $0.9 C_0 - m(t) < 0.512\text{ Mbps}$) or $9.0\text{ Mbps}$, $r$ falls below the $512\text{ kbps}$ safety floor (or becomes negative), causing the deterministic `SAFETYARBITER` (Section V-D, p. 5) to automatically veto `rate_limit` into `no_op` precisely when mice load is highest. Furthermore, the safety arbiter rejects any meter "discarding more than 20% of the measured flow rate" (requiring $r/1000 \ge 0.80 e(t)$). Because Line 3 caps $r/1000$ at $\min(0.9 C_0 - m(t), 0.85 e(t))$, any elephant burst where $0.9 C_0 - m(t) < 0.80 e(t)$ (e.g., $m(t) = 4\text{ Mbps}$ and $e(t) > 6.25\text{ Mbps}$) will synthesize a rate that discards more than 20% of $e(t)$, causing the safety arbiter to systematically veto `rate_limit` into `no_op` during large bursts rather than clamping the rate to the safety boundary. Clarifying this interaction (and reporting the veto counts referenced in Section V-D) would strengthen the algorithmic description.
   - **Non-Negativity of Penalty Weights $\mathbf{w}$ (Algorithm 2, Lines 6–8):** Line 6 clips each weight update to $\Delta w_k \leftarrow \operatorname{clip}(\rho.\Delta w_k, -0.15, 0.15)$, and Line 8 updates and normalizes the weights via $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow (\mathbf{w} + \Delta\mathbf{w}) / \|\mathbf{w} + \Delta\mathbf{w}\|_1$ without clamping individual components $w_k + \Delta w_k$ to be non-negative (unlike $V$, which is explicitly clamped to $[0.5, 32]$ in Line 7). Over multiple governance epochs—or whenever a weight component satisfies $w_k < 0.15$—negative deltas can drive $w_k + \Delta w_k < 0$ (and potentially allow $\|\mathbf{w} + \Delta\mathbf{w}\|_1 = 0$), which would invert the sign of the corresponding penalty term (such as delay or packet loss) into a reward in the fast-loop cost $c(a)$ and violate the non-negativity bound $c(t) \in [0, C_{\max}]$ required by Theorem 1. Clarifying whether element-wise non-negativity clipping $\max(0, \mathbf{w} + \Delta\mathbf{w})$ is enforced prior to $\ell_1$-normalization is recommended.

3. **Statistical Testing Methodology: Omitted Cluster Bootstrap Intervals, Pseudoreplication in Paired $t$-Tests, and Missing Comparisons in Table V (Section VII-G, Pages 7–8)**
   - **Potential Concern:** Section VII-G (page 7) and Table V (page 8) evaluate statistical significance by pairing observations across $n = 300$ matched `(seed, window)` pairs ($5\text{ seeds} \times 60\text{ windows per seed}$). Three methodological and reporting issues arise:
     1. *Missing Cluster Bootstrap Intervals in Table V:* Section VII-G states that *"Because windows inside a seed are serially dependent, Table V reports the paired $t$ statistic alongside a seed-level cluster bootstrap, and the bootstrap interval is what we rely on,"* and the Table V caption similarly states *"THE INTERVAL IS A SEED-LEVEL CLUSTER BOOTSTRAP."* However, Table V only displays the columns `Comparison`, `n`, `t`, `p`, and `Cohen's d`—the seed-level cluster bootstrap confidence intervals are omitted from the rendered table and text.
     2. *Serial Correlation and Pseudoreplication in Window-Level Parametric $t$-Tests:* Because queue backlogs $Q_i(t)$ and multi-window governance epochs ($T_g = 30\text{ s} = 6\text{ windows}$) induce strong positive autocorrelation across consecutive $5\text{ s}$ windows within the same $300\text{ s}$ seed trajectory, treating $n = 300$ windows as independent observations ($\text{df} = 299$ instead of $\text{df} = 4$ at the independent seed level) in standard parametric paired $t$-tests inflates the $t$-statistics ($8.880$ to $11.661$) and artificially yields parametric $p$-values of `< 10^{-12}`. Furthermore, with only $5$ independent clusters (seeds), a non-parametric seed-level cluster bootstrap has $5^5 = 3{,}125$ possible resamples (and $2^5 = 32$ sign-flips for paired seed means). Reporting the actual cluster-bootstrap confidence intervals in Table V alongside seed-level paired statistics ($n = 5$) is needed to substantiate the claims and align the table with its caption.
     3. *Omitted Comparisons vs. Bonferroni Threshold:* Section VII-G reports a Bonferroni-corrected family-wise significance threshold of $\alpha = 0.0063$, which corresponds to $0.05 / 8 = 0.00625$ (i.e., a family of 8 pairwise comparisons against `S5`: `S0`–`S4` plus the 3 external non-LLM baselines in Table IV). However, Table V only lists 5 comparisons (`S5 vs. S0`, `S5 vs. S1`, `S5 vs. S2`, `S5 vs. S4`, and `S5 vs. Q`), omitting `S5 vs. S3` (+LoRA) as well as `S5 vs. Capacity-aware threshold` and `S5 vs. Greedy least-loaded` (the latter of which achieves lower packet loss than `Tabular Q-learning` in Table IV).
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Add the missing seed-level cluster bootstrap confidence interval column (and/or seed-level paired test statistics across the $n = 5$ independent seeds) to Table V as described in the text and caption, and include the remaining 3 comparisons (`S5 vs. S3`, `S5 vs. Threshold`, and `S5 vs. Least-loaded`) so the table is complete and matches the 8-test Bonferroni denominator ($\alpha = 0.0063$).
