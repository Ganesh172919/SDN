### Summary

This paper presents SELF-X, an evaluation of a multi-timescale software-defined networking (SDN) congestion control architecture that combines a fast (100 ms) analytic drift-plus-penalty (DPP) controller with a medium-timescale (5 s) LoRA-adapted small language model (SmolLM2-135M-Instruct) action prior and a slow-timescale (30 s) multi-role language model objective governor. Using a shared two-path finite-buffer fluid simulator and twelve paired synthetic traffic traces across fourteen reference and ablation configurations, the authors decouple actuation cadence from model advice latency and compare the hybrid stack against static forwarding, heuristics, online tabular Q-learning, and frozen deep reinforcement learning baselines (DQN and two-agent IQL). The empirical results demonstrate that while faster (100 ms) analytic actuation substantially reduces mean delay relative to static forwarding (by 25.15%) and 5 s actuation (by 15.65%), the language model layers provide no incremental delay improvement over standalone fast DPP control ($p=0.0745$), with the frozen LoRA prior significantly degrading delay ($p=0.0054$), the governor repeating a single static weight update across all test traces, and the verbal critique arm revising zero proposals.


### Strengths

- **Rigorous Experimental Decoupling and Ablation Design:** The benchmark cleanly separates fast-loop analytical actuation cadence (100 ms vs. 5 s) from sequential language model inference delays (0.4 s per stage) across fourteen paired configurations, enabling precise isolation of each architectural layer's contribution.

- **Mathematically Consistent Fluid Model and Execution Auditing:** The finite-buffer fluid datapath equations strictly satisfy per-slot bit-volume conservation and employ an exact piecewise-linear clipped backlog integral for queueing delay, while execution traces and action hashes transparently distinguish raw model outputs, proposed actions, and guard-filtered applied actions.

- **Sound Stratified Statistical Methodology:** The evaluation accounts for heterogeneous variance across the three traffic profiles using a stratified variance estimator ($s_{j}^{2}/(9n_{j})$) with Welch–Satterthwaite $t$-approximations and controls family-wise error rates across paired comparisons via Holm's step-down procedure.

- **Exemplary Claim-Evidence Calibration and Transparency:** None of the claims exceed the empirical evidence; the authors candidly report negative and degenerate findings—including mode collapse in the 30 s governor, zero revisions by the critique layer, identical applied action trajectories between DQN and two-agent IQL, and the delay-versus-loss trade-off relative to neural baselines—providing a valuable methodological corrective against attributing fast analytic actuation gains to language model reasoning.


### Weaknesses

- **Incremental Architectural Novelty:** The multi-timescale control paradigm decoupling fast per-slot network actuation from slower language model governance, verbal reflection, and low-rank adaptation builds closely on established analytical optimization and recent LLM-networking frameworks (Reference Paper: Stochastic Network Optimization with Application to Communication and Queueing Systems, Michael J. Neely, 2010; Reference Paper: NetLLM: Adapting Large Language Models for Networking, Duo Wu et al., 2024; Reference Paper: Multi-Agent LLM Governance for Safe Two-Timescale Reinforcement Learning in SDN-IoT Defense, Saeid Jamshidi et al., 2026; Reference Paper: Reflection-Driven Self-Improving Language Model Agents for Adaptive RAN Slicing, Yuanhao Li et al., 2026).

- **Privileged One-Step Lookahead in the Fast Analytic Controller:** Because the fast DPP optimizer evaluates the three-action space ($\{P,A,M\}$) using exact current-slot arrival rates and the identical deterministic transition equations as the simulator datapath, it acts as an omniscient one-step lookahead solver, making external coarse-grained model preferences and uncalibrated proxy costs ($E(u),R(u)$) act primarily as perturbations to locally optimal one-step decisions.

- **Mode Collapse of the Sub-Billion-Parameter Language Model Layers:** Relying on an ultra-compact 135M-parameter model (SmolLM2-135M-Instruct) for quantitative telemetry interpretation and multi-role deliberation results in state-invariant behavior (100% identical governor ballot merges and 0 critique revisions out of 432 requests), and the authors might consider clarifying the exact telemetry inputs passed to the governor prompts as well as the mechanistic interaction between decaying $V$ and the fixed $+0.20$ LoRA preference margin (Reference Paper: SmolLM2: When Smol Goes Big — Data-Centric Training of a Small Language Model, Loubna Ben Allal et al., 2025; Reference Paper: Reflexion: Language Agents with Verbal Reinforcement Learning, Noah Shinn et al., 2023).

- **Objective Misalignment and Underspecified Baseline Interfaces:** The reinforcement learning training reward differs structurally from the analytic DPP objective by including a large rule-switching penalty and a $1.5\times $ higher relative weight on traffic loss while omitting energy/resource proxies, and several baseline specifications (such as the custom headroom rule for threshold/tabular metering and exact numerical parameters for the flash-crowd and volatile workloads) would benefit from explicit documentation in the text.

- **Simplified Simulation Scope and External Validity:** As candidly noted by the authors, the evaluation is restricted to a single two-path fluid topology without packet-level queueing dynamics, transport-layer congestion feedback, or physical OpenFlow switch rule-installation latencies, limiting direct generalization to production networks.

##### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

- **Verification Summary of Fluid Datapath Dynamics and Controller Formulation (Sections III and IV):** The mathematical formulation across Sections III and IV is self-consistent and dimensionally homogeneous:

- *Fluid Queue Transitions and Volume Conservation (Eqs. 1–3):* With initial backlogs $q_{i}\in [0,B]$ in $\text{Mbit}$ ($B=2\text{\ Mbit}$), constant intra-slot admitted path rates $a_{i}$ and capacities $c_{i}=10\text{\ Mbps}$, and slot duration $\tau =0.1\text{\ s}$, the unconstrained trajectory $q_{i}+(a_{i}-c_{i})u$ is monotonic over $u\in [0,\tau ]$ and cannot simultaneously cross both buffer boundaries ($0$ and $B$). Consequently, $q_{i}^{\prime }+o_{i}=[x_{i}]_{0}^{B}+[x_{i}-B]_{+}=\max (0,x_{i})$, which guarantees non-negative served volume $s_{i}=q_{i}+a_{i}\tau -\max (0,x_{i})=\min (c_{i}\tau ,q_{i}+a_{i}\tau )\in [0,c_{i}\tau ]$ across all saturation regimes and satisfies the exact per-slot bit conservation identity $\sum _{i}q_{i}+O=\sum _{i}(q_{i}^{\prime }+s_{i}+o_{i})+D_{\text{m}}$.

- *Clipped Backlog Integral and Fluid Delay Proxy (Eq. 4):* Because $a_{i}-c_{i}$ is constant within each $100\text{\ ms}$ slot and $q_{i}\in [0,B]$ at $u=0$, the piecewise-linear clipped integral $\={q}_{i}=\tau ^{-1}\int _{0}^{\tau }[q_{i}+(a_{i}-c_{i})u]_{0}^{B}\,du$ is exact in closed form. The scaling factor $1000\={q}_{i}/c_{i}$ properly converts $\text{Mbit}/\text{Mbps}$ (seconds) into milliseconds to match propagation delays $d_{i}\in \{2,12\}\text{\ ms}$, and the degenerate regimes for zero service ($S=0$) and zero offered load ($O=0$) are explicitly specified.

- *Fast DPP Objective and Guard Interface (Eqs. 5–7):* The one-step rate-form drift surrogate $\sum _{i}q_{i}(a_{i}(u)-c_{i})$ (evaluated with exact one-step lookahead of the current slot's deterministic fluid transition and absorbing $\tau =0.1\text{\ s}$ into the penalty weight $V=8$), the penalty score $C(u)$, the model-preference bias $\text{round}(J(u),6)+0.20\,\mathbf{1}(u\ne h)$, and the deterministic tie-breaking order ($P,A,M$) are well specified.

- **Potential Concern — Unspecified Meter Ceiling Formula ("Headroom Rule") for Threshold and Tabular Baselines and Guard Interaction (Section IV-A):** Section IV-A defines the closed-form local ingress meter ceiling $r$ for the DPP, direct-model, and neural controllers in Equation (5): $r=\frac{\lfloor 1000\min \{\max (0.512,9-m),\max (0.85e,0.512)\}\rfloor }{1000}$ and immediately notes: *"The threshold and tabular arms use their own headroom rule and still pass the same guard."* While Section V-A later describes the conditions under which the capacity-threshold heuristic proposes metering, neither Section IV-A nor Section V-A states the mathematical formula used by the capacity-threshold and tabular Q-learning arms to compute the scalar meter rate $r$ (in Mbps), nor does Section V-A clarify whether `Least loaded` strictly selects from $\{P,A\}$ or can also propose $M$. Because $r$ directly determines whether action $M$ passes the shared guard conditions ($r\ge 0.512\text{\ Mbps}$ and $\max (0,1-r/e)\le 0.20$) as well as the admitted bulk rate $\~{e}=\min (e,r)$ and metered volume $D_{\text{m}}=(e-\~{e})\tau $ in Eq. (3), omitting this expression is a potential concern for standalone reproducibility and baseline parity assessment without inspecting the external code artifact. Additionally, under Eq. (5), whenever $9-m<0.80e$ (for instance, at the nominal background demand $m=3\text{\ Mbps}$ when bulk demand $e>7.5\text{\ Mbps}$), capping $r$ at $\approx 9-m$ violates the guard condition $1-r/e\le 0.20$, causing $M$ to be guarded out during large bursts (such as the $8\text{--}15\text{\ Mbps}$ bursts in Section V-B); briefly noting this intentional restriction alongside the explicit headroom formula for the threshold and tabular baselines would make Section IV-A self-contained.

- **Potential Concern — Underspecified Telemetry Inputs for the 30 s Governor and Direct Base-Model Prompts (Section IV-B):** Section IV-B clearly specifies the input representation and output space for the $5\text{\ s}$ LoRA adapter (*"selects one of three constrained tokens from a snapshot of the two demand rates rounded to 0.5 Mbps"*). A second prompt merges their ballots into one of three choices..."*), the manuscript does not state what numerical telemetry or summary statistics (e.g., instantaneous or window-averaged queue backlogs $q_{i}$, realized delay $D$, loss $\ell $, goodput $G$, or active weights $(V,\mathbf{w})$) are passed into the five role prompts, nor what state inputs are provided to the direct base-model arms ($S_{1},S_{2}$). Given the central empirical finding in Section VI-c that all 360 governor ballots and 72 merges selected the exact same queue-and-loss adjustment across the test set, briefly specifying the state variables formatted into the governor and base-model prompts is a substantive consideration so readers can distinguish whether the static output reflects model insensitivity to changing numerical telemetry or an invariant prompt structure.

- **Potential Concern — Boundary Details in Asynchronous Advice Evaluation and Governor Weight Renormalization (Sections IV-A and IV-B):** The multi-timescale interface between the $100\text{\ ms}$ datapath loop, the $5\text{\ s}$ LoRA adapter ($0.4\text{\ s}$ delay), and the $30\text{\ s}$ governor ($0.8\text{\ s}$ delay) is well structured, but three minor boundary specifications represent potential concerns for unambiguous reproducibility:

- *Dynamic per-slot vs. on-arrival feasibility check of stored preference $h$:* Section IV-A states that *"A feasible model preference $h$ changes the selection to $\arg \min _{u}[\text{round}(J(u),6)+0.20\,\mathbf{1}(u\ne h)]$; otherwise it uses the unrounded DPP minimum."* Because the fast loop runs every $100\text{\ ms}$ while $h\in \{P,A,M\}$ is queried every $5\text{\ s}$ from a $0.5\text{\ Mbps}$-rounded demand snapshot and persists across fifty $100\text{\ ms}$ slots, instantaneous demand $(m,e)$ and the local meter ceiling $r$ can fluctuate within that $5\text{\ s}$ window such that a stored preference $h$ satisfies the shared guard in some $100\text{\ ms}$ slots and violates it in others. Clarifying whether the feasibility of $h$ is re-evaluated dynamically against each $100\text{\ ms}$ slot's current guard conditions (and confirming that an infeasible $h$ falls back to the unrounded DPP minimum rather than resolving $h$ to $P$ first) would remove any ambiguity.

- *Initial slots before the first advice arrives ($t\in [0,0.4)\text{\ s}$):* Section IV-B notes that the LoRA preference *"becomes available after 0.4 simulated seconds; the prior preference remains active until then."* Explicitly confirming that no prior preference $h$ is active during the first four $100\text{\ ms}$ slots of an episode before the $t=0\text{\ s}$ request completes (so the fast loop uses the unrounded DPP minimum) would make the initialization completely clear.

- *Governor weight renormalization under repeated boundary clipping:* On a governor update, the controller adds $\pm \Delta \mathbf{w}$ where $\Delta \mathbf{w}=(0.06,0.06,0,-0.07,-0.05)$ to $(w_{d},w_{\ell },w_{g},w_{E},w_{R})$, *"clips weights to nonnegative values, and renormalizes them on an update."* Because the components of $\Delta \mathbf{w}$ sum to zero and the initial weights $(0.25,0.25,0.25,0.15,0.10)$ sum to $1.00$, the sum remains $1.00$ until repeated $(-2,\Delta \mathbf{w})$ updates (which occur at every governor step in Section VI-c) clip $w_{R}$ (after 2 updates) and $w_{E}$ (after 3 updates) at zero. Explicitly writing the update step as $\~{\mathbf{w}}=\max (\mathbf{0},\mathbf{w}\pm \Delta \mathbf{w})$ followed by $\mathbf{w}\leftarrow \~{\mathbf{w}}/\|\~{\mathbf{w}}\|_{1}$ would make the exact weight trajectory across subsequent updates self-contained.

#### [Baselines, Experimental Protocol, and Statistical Methodology] (Pages: 2-3)


##### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

- **Potential Concern: Objective and Penalty Misalignment Between RL Training Rewards and the Analytic DPP Objective (Section V-A).** In Section V-A (Page 3, Column 1), the per-slot training reward for `DQN` and `two-agent IQL` (as well as the windowed version used for online `Tabular Q-learning` and the `S2` critique prompt) is defined as: $R_{\text{slot}}=\frac{G}{10}-\frac{D}{50}-1.5\ell -0.05(50I),$ where $I\in \{0,1\}$ indicates whether the effective forwarding or metering rule changed. Comparing this reward against the fast analytic optimizer's objective $J(u)=\sum _{i}q_{i}(a_{i}(u)-c_{i})+VC(u)$ and penalty function $C(u)=w_{d}D/50+w_{\ell }\ell -w_{g}G/10+w_{E}E(u)+w_{R}R(u)$ in Equations (6)–(7) reveals three structural differences in what the two controller families optimize:

- *Rule-switching penalty ($I$):* The RL reward explicitly penalizes every effective rule transition by $0.05\times 50=2.5$ (equivalent in magnitude to $125\text{\ ms}$ of single-slot delay), whereas the DPP objective $J(u)$ contains no switching cost term (relying only on the $+0.20\,\mathbf{1}(u\ne h)$ hysteresis toward the slow LoRA preference $h$ when active). This directly incentivizes the neural policies to suppress rule changes, aligning with the downstream observation in Section VI-b that `DQN` and `IQL` average $24.8$ effective rule changes per trace compared to $106.2$ for `DPP only` and $130.6$ for `S5`.

- *Relative delay-versus-loss weighting:* Scaling the initial DPP penalty $C(u)$ by $1/w_{d}=4$ at $(w_{d},w_{\ell },w_{g})=(0.25,0.25,0.25)$ weights scaled goodput ($-G/10$), scaled delay ($D/50$), and loss fraction ($\ell $) in a $1:1:1$ ratio, and the governor update vector $\Delta \mathbf{w}=(0.06,0.06,0,-0.07,-0.05)$ preserves $w_{d}=w_{\ell }$. In contrast, the RL reward weights $(G/10,-D/50,-\ell )$ at $(1.0,1.0,1.5)$, placing $50\%$ greater relative weight on loss avoidance than on scaled delay or goodput.

- *Static action-cost proxies ($E(u),R(u)$):* The DPP penalty includes static energy and resource proxies that penalize alternate routing $A$ ($E(A)=1.70,R(A)=0.55$) and metering $M$ ($E(M)=0.35,R(M)=0.40$) relative to primary forwarding $P$ ($E(P)=0.35,R(P)=0$), whereas the RL reward omits both proxies. While Section IV-A notes that *"The learning reward in Sec. V is separate,"* this objective divergence is a potential confounder when interpreting the empirical trade-off in Table II and Section VI-b (where `DQN` and `IQL` achieve $0.33$ percentage points lower loss and over $4\times $ fewer rule changes, while `S5` achieves $0.75\text{\ ms}$ lower mean delay). Explicitly discussing in Section V-A how the $1.5\ell $ weight and $-2.5I$ switching penalty shape the neural baselines' operating point would clarify that behavioral differences reflect reward/cost parameterization in addition to algorithmic differences.

- **Potential Concern: Incomplete Specification of Baseline State Representations and Two-Agent IQL Action Resolution (Section V-A).** Although the code artifact accompanies the manuscript, several baseline interface details in Section V-A are not fully specified in the text for standalone mathematical reproducibility and baseline parity assessment:

- *State discretization, normalization, and exploration schedules:* Section V-A notes that `Tabular Q-learning` uses *"bucketed demand and backlogs"* with initial exploration $0.20$, `DQN` and `IQL` observe *"normalized demand, both backlogs and the previous applied action,"* and Section V-B notes that LoRA training uses *"13 training states,"* without stating the bucket boundaries, normalization constants, or whether tabular exploration decays during a $180\text{\ s}$ test episode.

- *Two-agent IQL action spaces and voting mechanics:* Section V-A states that *"Each IQL agent sees the same state and receives the same reward; one votes for routing and one for metering, with rerouting taking precedence."* Clarifying each agent's exact action set (e.g., binary $\{P,A\}$ and $\{P,M\}$ votes versus full $\{P,A,M\}$ Q-values) and how exploratory actions are combined and stored in their respective replay buffers during training would make the two-agent formulation unambiguous and help explain why its frozen greedy policy converges to the exact same applied action trajectory as single-agent `DQN`.

- **Potential Concern: Timing and Hold Interactions in the Slow-Cadence Ablations (`S1` vs. `S2` and `S4` vs. `S5`) (Section V-A).**

- *Critique application delay asymmetry (`S1` vs. `S2`):* In Section V-A, `S1` (Base language model) applies its proposed action after a $0.4\text{\ s}$ simulated single-stage model delay, whereas `S2` (Base model plus critique) adds a sequential critique pass and applies the final action after $0.8\text{\ s}$. Because the fast datapath continues executing under the previous action while advice is pending, `S1` and `S2` differ simultaneously in the presence of the critique pass and in actuation latency ($0.4\text{\ s}$ vs. $0.8\text{\ s}$). While Section VI-c transparently notes that `S2` revises $0$ of $432$ proposals—meaning the slight mean delay difference between `S1` ($61.30\text{\ ms}$) and `S2` ($61.34\text{\ ms}$) arises solely from the extra $0.4\text{\ s}$ application delay—noting in Section V-A that this $0.4\text{\ s}$ offset is modeled as an inherent cost of sequential reflection (rather than comparing against a delay-matched $0.8\text{\ s}$ `S1` baseline) would clarify the ablation design earlier.

- *Held proposals and per-slot guard checks in `S4`:* Section V-A states that *"S4 uses the same model schedule as S5 but holds each DPP proposal for 5 s, subject to per-slot guard checks; delayed advice can affect its next DPP decision."* When `S4` (or `S1`–`S3`, `Capacity threshold`, and `Tabular Q`) holds a metering proposal $M$ across fifty $100\text{\ ms}$ slots, it is unclear whether the numerical meter ceiling $r$ is held fixed at its initial value for $5\text{\ s}$ or recomputed in each $100\text{\ ms}$ slot via Equation (5) from current demand $(m,e)$ prior to running the per-slot guard check $\max (0,1-r/e)\le 0.20$, and whether a guard override to $P$ applies only to the violating slot or latches for the remainder of the $5\text{\ s}$ window. In addition, because model advice arrives $0.4\text{\ s}$ (LoRA) or $0.8\text{\ s}$ (governor) after being requested at a $5\text{\ s}$ boundary, `S4` waits an additional $4.6\text{\ s}$ or $4.2\text{\ s}$ before that advice influences a DPP decision, whereas `S5` incorporates it at the immediately following $100\text{\ ms}$ slot.

- **Potential Concern: Two-Stage Windowed Delay Aggregation and End-of-Episode Backlog Accounting (Section V-B).** In Section V-B (Page 3, Column 1), two metric accounting conventions warrant brief clarification regarding their potential impact on comparative evaluation:

- *Unweighted average of volume-weighted window delays:* A seed's mean delay is defined as *"the arithmetic mean of its 36 five-second windows, with slot delays within each window weighted by served volume,"* whereas seed loss and goodput are computed from total $180\text{\ s}$ volumes ($\sum L/\sum O$ and $\sum S/180\text{\ s}$). Under non-stationary workloads such as the flash-crowd profile (where heavy bursts concentrate in the 30–70% interval), five-second windows have substantially different total served volumes. Averaging the $36$ window means with equal weight ($1/36$ each) gives low-volume baseline windows ($3\text{\ Mbps}$) the same influence on a seed's mean delay as high-volume burst windows ($11\text{\textendash }18\text{\ Mbps}$), differing from the episode-wide volume-weighted mean delay ($\sum _{t=1}^{1800}S_{t}D_{t}/\sum _{t=1}^{1800}S_{t}$). Clarifying the rationale for this two-stage windowed delay estimand would strengthen the metric specification.

- *Unaccounted end-of-episode backlog:* Section V-B specifies that *"Traffic still queued at the end is neither served nor counted as loss."* With two paths of buffer capacity $B=2\text{\ Mbit}$ each, up to $2B=4\text{\ Mbit}$ of traffic can remain queued at $t=180\text{\ s}$. Across a $180\text{\ s}$ episode offering roughly $1600\text{\textendash }1800\text{\ Mbit}$ of total volume, $4\text{\ Mbit}$ corresponds to roughly $0.22\text{\textendash }0.25\%$ of total offered volume—a bound comparable in magnitude to the $0.33$ percentage-point loss difference reported between `S5` ($24.88\%$) and `DQN`/`IQL` ($24.55\%$) and larger than the $0.01\text{\textendash }0.02$ percentage-point differences among the fast ablations in Table II. Confirming that residual queue backlog at $t=180\text{\ s}$ does not systematically differ across arms would verify that boundary truncation does not affect the secondary loss and goodput comparisons.

- **Potential Concern: Incomplete Numerical Parameterization of the Flash-Crowd and Volatile Traffic Profiles (Section V-B).** In Section V-B (Page 3, Column 1, Paragraph 1), the steady-bursty traffic profile is quantitatively specified with exact parameters ($15\text{\ s}$ mean exponential inter-burst spacing, $8\text{\textendash }15\text{\ Mbps}$ burst rate, $4\text{\textendash }10\text{\ s}$ burst duration), but the other two profiles are described only qualitatively: the flash-crowd profile is described as having *"more frequent and larger bursts in the 30–70% interval,"* and the volatile profile as updating *"every 0.5 s with two sinusoidal components and uniform noise."* Providing the exact numerical parameters (inter-arrival mean, burst rate/duration ranges inside and outside the 30–70% interval, sinusoidal amplitudes/periods, and uniform noise bounds) would make the workload characterization self-contained.

#### [Empirical Results, Ablation Analysis, and Claim-Evidence Consistency] (Pages: 1, 3-4)


##### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

- **Non-Additive Interaction Between Governor $V$-Decay and the Fixed LoRA Preference Margin, and Directionality of the Ablation Summary (Abstract, Page 1; Table II and Figure 1, Page 3; Section VI-c and Section VII, Page 4):**

- *Potential Concern:* In Section I, the second research question asks: *"Do the action prior and objective governor add value to fast analytic control?"* In the Abstract and Section VII, the findings for these two components are summarized jointly as showing *"no demonstrated incremental delay benefit from the combined language model layers"* and *"no evidence of a delay benefit over fast DPP alone."* While accurate for the combined stack (`S5` vs. `DPP only`), summarizing only the joint effect obscures a statistically significant component-level finding and an unexamined, opposing interaction across the four $100\text{\ ms}$ analytic configurations in Table II:

- `DPP only` (neither LoRA nor governor): $60.42\text{\ ms}$ delay, $24.57\%$ loss, $6.646\text{\ Mbps}$ goodput

- `S5 without governor` (DPP + LoRA): $60.41\text{\ ms}$ delay, $24.58\%$ loss, $6.644\text{\ Mbps}$ goodput

- `S5 without LoRA` (DPP + governor): $60.07\text{\ ms}$ delay, $24.56\%$ loss, $6.647\text{\ Mbps}$ goodput

- `S5 SELF-X` (DPP + LoRA + governor): $60.54\text{\ ms}$ delay, $24.88\%$ loss, $6.621\text{\ Mbps}$ goodput Without the governor, adding the LoRA prior to fast DPP has almost no net effect on mean delay or loss (`S5 without governor` at $60.41\text{\ ms}$ vs. `DPP only` at $60.42\text{\ ms}$). Without LoRA, adding the governor's deterministic update schedule to fast DPP reduces mean delay by $0.35\text{\ ms}$ without increasing loss (`S5 without LoRA` at $60.07\text{\ ms}$ vs. `DPP only` at $60.42\text{\ ms}$, achieving the lowest mean delay among all fourteen configurations). However, combining both layers in `S5 SELF-X` degrades delay by $0.48\text{\ ms}$ relative to `S5 without LoRA` (Holm-adjusted $p=0.0054$) and increases traffic loss by $0.32$ percentage points ($24.88\%$ vs. $24.56\%$).
